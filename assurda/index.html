Their first computer

Asus is donating 100 desktop PCs to an NGO. Many of the people who will use them have never touched a computer, and power and internet come and go. Which free operating system should they run?

Who we’re choosing for

100identical desktops, installed once and used for years
1stcomputer ever for many users, with no Windows habits to fall back on
Lowpower and internet: electricity drops, connections are slow or missing

So the best system isn’t the most powerful one. It’s the one people can’t get lost in, and that keeps working when the internet doesn’t.

About 100 in, 3 out

We started from a long list of about a hundred free systems, mostly Linux distributions, and asked four questions.

  1. Is it free and open?Out: ChromeOS Flex. Free to use, but closed and built around Google services.
  2. Is it still updated?Out: dozens of small or abandoned projects. Without security updates a PC is a risk.
  3. Can a beginner use it?Out: Arch, Gentoo, FreeBSD. Great for experts, not for someone who has never held a mouse.
  4. Is it the best of its kind?Out: Ubuntu, Lubuntu, Puppy Linux. Good systems, but a finalist does the same job better.

Finalist 1 of 3

Linux Mint

The familiar one

Menu in the corner, taskbar at the bottom, windows with the usual buttons. If these users later work in an office, what they learned still counts.

Good at

  • Looks like Windows
  • Stable and well tested
  • Huge community, easy to find help

Watch out

  • Expects internet for apps and updates
  • Heavier than a Lite system

Zorin OS Lite

The light one

Built on Xfce, one of the lightest desktops around, so it stays quick on modest PCs. It looks familiar too, with a simple start menu and taskbar.

Good at

  • Very light and fast
  • Familiar, uncluttered layout

Watch out

  • Zorin OS 18 is the last Lite edition, supported until June 2029
  • Smaller community
  • Needs internet for most apps

Finalist 3 of 3

Endless OS

The offline one

Made for homes and schools with little or no internet. An encyclopedia, learning content and apps come already installed, on a home screen that works like a phone.

Good at

  • Works offline from day one
  • The core system can’t be broken by accident
  • Updates and apps can travel by USB stick

Watch out

  • Big download, up to about 13 GB
  • Less like an office PC
  • Apps only from its App Center

2 GBof RAM is enough for all three

A new Asus desktop runs any of them easily. The biggest savings come from sleep and screen-dimming settings, which all three have. Mint also comes in a lighter Xfce edition.

Accessibility

Zoom and big text

Zoom, large text and high-contrast themes for people who can’t see well.

Screen reader

Orca reads the screen aloud for people who can’t see at all.

On-screen keyboard

Plus keyboard shortcuts, for anyone who struggles with a mouse.

Many languages

Dozens of languages, right-to-left scripts included.

All three have these. Mint and Endless make them easiest to find.

Our pick

Endless OS

  • Works offline. Encyclopedia, lessons and apps are already on the PC.
  • Hard to break. Updates are all-or-nothing, and the previous version is kept.
  • Made for beginners. A phone-like home screen for people new to computers.

Plan B Linux Mint, wherever the internet is reliable and office skills matter.

Fun fact

IBM z/OS

The heaviest system to run

z/OS is IBM’s operating system for its Z mainframes, the machines built for big business transactions. On top of the machine, IBM charges for the system every month.
