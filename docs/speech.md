# Speaker script – Which free OS for 100 Asus PCs?

## Come usarlo

Ognuno di voi parla circa 3 minuti: ogni parte è di 350–400 parole, da dire con calma. Le righe **[Slide N]** indicano quando cambiare slide.

- I vostri nomi sono già scritti nel discorso e nella prima e nell'ultima slide.
- Non leggete parola per parola: imparate a memoria la prima e l'ultima frase della vostra parte e usate il resto come traccia.
- Le frasi in **grassetto** sono quelle chiave: rallentate e guardate il pubblico.
- Il passaggio al compagno è già nell'ultima frase di ogni parte.
- Provate tutti insieme con un timer: circa 12 minuti in totale, più le domande.

## Speaker 1 – Lino Maini · slides 1–3

*The challenge and how we chose · about 3 minutes*

**[Slide 1 · Their first computer]**

Good morning, everyone. Think back to the first time you used a computer. Someone probably sat next to you, showed you where to click, and helped when something went wrong.

Now imagine that moment in a village school or a small clinic, where nobody has used a computer before, the power sometimes goes off, and the internet is slow or not there at all.

That is the situation behind our project. Asus is donating one hundred desktop computers to an NGO, and the company asked us a simple question: which free operating system should we install on them?

I'm Lino, and together with Davide, Dennis and Roberto, I'm going to give you our answer.

**[Slide 2 · Who we're choosing for]**

First, let's be clear about who we are choosing for.

One hundred identical desktops. They will be installed once and used for years, often far away from any technician.

For many users, this will be their first computer. They have no Windows habits to fall back on. If something is confusing, they simply get stuck.

And the conditions are hard: electricity can drop, and the internet may be slow, expensive or missing.

**So the best system is not the most powerful one. It's the one people can't get lost in, and that keeps working when the internet doesn't.**

**[Slide 3 · About 100 in, 3 out]**

So how did we choose? We started from a long list of about one hundred free systems, most of them Linux distributions, and we asked four questions.

One: is it really free and open? ChromeOS Flex costs nothing, but it's closed and built around Google services, so it was out.

Two: is it still updated? Dozens of small or abandoned projects dropped out here, because a computer without security updates is a risk.

Three: can a beginner use it? Arch, Gentoo and FreeBSD are great for experts, not for someone who has never held a mouse.

Four: is it the best of its kind? Ubuntu, Lubuntu and Puppy Linux are good systems, but each time one of our finalists did the same job better.

**Three systems survived, and each one wins in its own way: the familiar one, the light one and the offline one.** Davide will introduce them.

## Speaker 2 – Davide Miron · slides 4–6

*The three finalists · about 3 minutes*

**[Slide 4 · Linux Mint]**

Thank you, Lino. Let's start with the familiar one: Linux Mint.

Look at the picture: a menu in the corner, a taskbar at the bottom, windows with the usual buttons. It looks a lot like Windows, and that's the point. If these users later work in an office, what they learned will still be useful.

Mint is known for being stable and well tested, and it has one of the biggest communities in the Linux world, so help is easy to find.

It needs 2 gigabytes of memory, 4 recommended, and 20 gigabytes of disk, and each version gets updates for about five years.

**The weak point: Mint expects an internet connection to install apps and updates.**

**[Slide 5 · Zorin OS Lite]**

Our second candidate is the light one: Zorin OS Lite.

It's built on Xfce, one of the lightest desktops around, so it stays quick on modest computers and asks less from the hardware. It looks familiar too, with a simple start menu and taskbar.

But our research found an important detail. **Zorin says version 18 is the last one with a Lite edition.** It's supported until June 2029; after that, users would have to move to a heavier edition. Its community is also smaller than Mint's and, like Mint, it needs the internet for most apps.

**[Slide 6 · Endless OS]**

Our third candidate is the offline one: Endless OS.

Endless was designed for exactly the people we're talking about: homes and schools with little or no internet. It comes with an encyclopedia, learning content and many apps already inside, so the computer is useful from the very first day, without any connection. The home screen is a grid of icons, like a smartphone, which many people already know.

There's another advantage: users can't change the core system, so it's very hard to break by accident. If an update fails, the previous version is still there. Updates and apps can even be copied from one PC to another with a USB stick.

The downsides: the full version is a big download, up to about 13 gigabytes. It looks less like an office PC, and apps come only from its own App Center.

So, three good options. Which one is best? Dennis will compare them.

## Speaker 3 – Dennis Boschi · slides 7–9

*Head to head · about 3 minutes*

**[Slide 7 · How they score]**

Thanks, Davide. To compare the three, we scored each one from one to five on five criteria. These are our own ratings, based on the official documentation and on reviews.

Easy on day one: Endless gets a five, thanks to its phone-like home screen.

Works without internet: Endless gets a five again. The other two need a connection for most apps and updates.

Light on energy: Zorin OS Lite is the clear winner.

Accessibility: Mint and Endless are a little more polished than Zorin Lite.

Future updates: Mint is the safest. Zorin's Lite edition ends after 2029.

**Adding it up: Endless 20 out of 25, Mint 18, Zorin Lite 16.** Endless leads exactly where it matters most for these users.

**[Slide 8 · Energy]**

Let's look more closely at energy, because power is a real problem in many places.

A lighter desktop asks less from the processor and the memory, so it uses a bit less electricity. Xfce, in Zorin Lite, is the lightest. Cinnamon, in Mint, is in the middle. Endless uses a customised GNOME desktop, the heaviest of the three. And if energy became the main problem, Mint also comes in a lighter Xfce edition.

**But here's the honest part: all three run on 2 gigabytes of memory, and a new Asus desktop handles any of them easily.** The biggest savings come from settings like sleep mode and screen dimming, and all three have them.

**[Slide 9 · Accessibility]**

Finally, accessibility. A donation should work for everyone, not only for people with perfect sight or steady hands.

If someone can't see well, all three offer zoom, large text and high-contrast themes.

If someone can't see at all, the Orca screen reader reads the screen aloud.

If someone struggles with a mouse, there's an on-screen keyboard and keyboard shortcuts.

And if someone doesn't read English, all three are available in dozens of languages, including right-to-left scripts like Arabic.

**Mint and Endless make these settings easiest to find.** So, after all this, what do we recommend? Roberto will tell you.

## Speaker 4 – Roberto Cacace · slides 10–12

*Our pick and the plan · about 3 minutes*

**[Slide 10 · Our pick]**

Thank you, Dennis. **Our recommendation for Asus is Endless OS.** There are three reasons.

First, it's useful on day one, with no internet at all: the encyclopedia, the learning content and the apps are already on the computer.

Second, it's hard to break. Users can't damage the core system by accident, updates are all-or-nothing, and the previous version is kept as a backup.

Third, it was made for exactly these users: people meeting a computer for the first time, in places with poor connections. Think back to the person we described at the start. Of our three finalists, Endless is the only one designed for exactly that person.

Do we have a plan B? Yes. **Where the internet is reliable and people need office skills, Linux Mint is an excellent choice.**

**[Slide 11 · From box to classroom]**

A good choice also needs a good plan. This is how we would set it up.

Step one: prepare one USB stick with Endless OS in the local language and with the apps the NGO needs.

Step two: install that same setup on all one hundred PCs. Endless has a special "reformatter" USB stick for exactly this job.

Step three: train five to ten local people as computer champions. They learn first, then they help everyone else.

Step four: keep the computers updated. One PC with internet downloads the updates, and a USB stick carries them to the others.

And the cost of the software for all one hundred computers? Zero.

**[Slide 12 · Thank you]**

To sum up: we compared three free systems. Linux Mint, the familiar one. Zorin OS Lite, the light one. And Endless OS, the offline one.

For people using a computer for the very first time, in places where the internet isn't guaranteed, we believe Endless OS is the right choice, **because a computer should keep working even when the internet doesn't.**

One last thought: Asus isn't just giving away one hundred machines. With the right system, it's giving one hundred communities a first window onto the world's knowledge.

Thank you for listening. We're happy to answer your questions.

## Likely questions

Short answers anyone in the group can give.

| Question | Short answer |
| --- | --- |
| Why not Windows? | It isn't free: 100 licences cost money, and it needs regular internet for updates. The brief also asked for a free system. |
| Why not Ubuntu? | Ubuntu is good, but Linux Mint is built on it and has a more familiar layout, so for beginners Mint does the same job better. |
| Won't they need Windows skills for jobs? | The basics (mouse, keyboard, files, browser) are the same everywhere. Where office skills matter and the internet is good, our plan B is Mint. |
| Can Endless open Word or Excel files? | Yes, with a free office app such as LibreOffice from its App Center. |
| How do you add apps without internet? | Many are already installed. Others can be copied from another Endless PC with a USB stick. |
| Is Endless OS still alive? | Yes: version 6 got updates in 2026, and Endless OS 7 is being built. |
| What about viruses? | Viruses for Linux are rare, and the Endless core system is read-only, so it is very hard to damage. |
| Why not Zorin OS Lite, if it's the lightest? | Its Lite edition ends after 2029, it needs internet for most apps, and on new Asus PCs the energy difference is small. |
